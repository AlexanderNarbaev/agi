# Coastal erosion and the loss of marsh land

Erosion of the shoreline of a coastal plain removes the sediment that the marsh needs to accrete.

A seawall without a marsh in front of it reflects the incoming wave energy back onto the shore.

A restored marsh in front of a seawall dissipates wave energy and reduces the erosion of the shoreline.

The width of the marsh belt is the main factor that controls how much wave energy reaches the shoreline.

Relative sea level rise drowns the marsh platform where the sediment supply cannot keep pace.

A marsh that loses its creek network reduces the exchange of water and sediment with the estuary.

Dredging of a navigation channel increases the tidal prism and deepens the water beside the marsh.

A storm surge causes new sediment to be transported onto the surface of a low marsh.

The landward migration of a marsh requires space that is free of fixed structures.

A levee that isolates a marsh from the floodplain reduces the supply of the river sediment the marsh needs.

Vegetation loss on a marsh platform increases the exposure of the soil to wave erosion.

The rate of marsh loss along a developed shoreline is higher than the rate of marsh gain on a sheltered one.
