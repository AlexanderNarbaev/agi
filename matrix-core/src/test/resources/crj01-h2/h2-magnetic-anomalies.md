# Magnetic anomalies in the ocean floor

A magnetic anomaly is the difference between the field measured at a site and the field a dipole would produce there. The pattern of alternating polarity in the ocean floor is a record of the reversals of the Earth's magnetic field. Reversals of the Earth's magnetic field have occurred at irregular intervals over the past two hundred million years. A marine magnetic anomaly survey measures the magnetic field along a ship track across the ocean floor.

The youngest anomalies occur close to the ridge axis. The older anomalies occur farther from the ridge axis. The width of a band of matching polarity increases with the age of the crust it was created in. A magnetometer measures the intensity of the magnetic field at the Earth's surface. Shipboard surveys of magnetic anomalies provided the first continuous profile of the ocean floor.

Paleomagnetic measurements of cooling lava reveal the direction of the field at the time the lava hardened. The polarity sequence of the anomalies forms a mirror image on the two sides of a mid-ocean ridge. Symmetric bands of matching polarity on both flanks of a ridge provide the central evidence for seafloor spreading. The spacing of the anomaly bands supports a rate of seafloor spreading of a few centimetres per year.

A magnetometer of the type used on research vessels can detect a field change of a few nanoTesla. The anomaly measured over a reversing segment has the opposite sign to the field over the segment beside it. A long survey line across the ocean floor gives the complete sequence of reversals in one measurement. The polarities of the anomalies correspond to the periods of normal and reversed field that the record of geomagnetic reversals describes. The narrowest band of a given polarity belongs to the most recent interval of the reversed field. The contribution of a single reversal to the width of the anomaly band is a function of the spreading rate and the duration of the interval. A survey of the anomalies of the Pacific basin requires a track of several thousand kilometres of ship time.
